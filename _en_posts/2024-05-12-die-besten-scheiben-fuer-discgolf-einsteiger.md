For newcomers to disc golf, buying your first discs can be very complicated. What matters, and which mistakes should you avoid? In this post, we try to shed some light on the subject and put together a few beginner sets for you at the end.

If you'd like to get into the game in general first, our [guide for disc golf beginners](/en/blog/leitfaden-fuer-einsteiger/) is a good companion read.

## Midrange, understable, overmold … come again?

First of all, let's clear up a few terms. Generally speaking, there are three types of discs: putters, midranges and drivers. Of course, that's only half the truth, and advanced players know that there are many subdivisions within each category. For now, though, you don't need to worry about that – you just need to understand how these discs differ and what their pros and cons are.

Just as traditional golf with a ball has different clubs, disc golf has different discs for different distances. Some terms such as driver and putter have been borrowed, while others are used exclusively in disc sports.

![Three different disc golf discs: driver, midrange, putter](/assets/images/posts/driver-mid-putter.jpg)

The picture above shows these three most important discs. At the top, in turquoise, is a **driver**. With its very sharp edge, it is made to fly fast through the air and cover distance. These discs have very low air resistance, so they reach the highest speeds. Pros throw them more than 120 metres, but beginners find it hard to control these discs and get them heading towards the target.

In contrast, the grey disc at the bottom is much less aerodynamic and wants to be thrown slowly. In return, it can be thrown much more precisely and easily. This type is called a **putter** and is used by disc golfers for very short distances.

The orange disc in the middle is the step between driver and putter and is called a **midrange**. These discs fly further than a putter and are still very easy to control, but don't reach the speeds of a driver.

> A putter and a midrange are enough to get started. Learn to master these discs before you buy more.

The chart above shows the approximate flight paths of the different discs when thrown backhand by a right-hander. The value at the bottom, at the start of the flight path, indicates the disc's speed. When you shop for discs, you'll come across these numbers again. Values like `2|3|0|0` for a putter or `7|5|0|2` for a driver show you the approximate flight characteristics of each disc. For now, we'll focus on the first value, which indicates the speed of the disc.

For beginners, slow discs (putters, midranges) are easier to control and help you learn the disc golf throwing motion. Discs with a speed below 6 are recommended here. Try to throw these discs 70 metres before you move on to the next category (drivers). Fast discs only fly further if you have the right technique. Most beginners don't, so discs with a speed above 9 tend to lead to frustration rather than more birdies in the first few months.

As a general rule, "understable" discs are more suitable for beginners. We'll skip exactly what that means in this post – the list at the end contains suitable discs, so you don't need to worry about it.

## Not all plastic is created equal

![Two disc golf discs with signs of wear](/assets/images/posts/premium-plastic.jpg)

Cheap discs are made from types of plastic that aren't particularly durable. That's why, even as a beginner, it's worth spending a few euros more and going for "premium plastic".

This picture shows how hits from trees or rocks affect discs. The red disc is made of VIP plastic from Westside Discs and shows almost no signs of use, whereas the blue putter was made from D-Line plastic (Discmania) and is now very battered. There are use cases for cheap plastic, but for beginners we recommend premium plastic for discs with a speed higher than three.

## How many discs do you need at the start?

Depending on your budget for the new hobby, we recommend starting with two to four discs. A putter for the last throw into the basket, which should feel good in your hand. Plus a putter in premium plastic for approaches from 20 to 60 metres. If you want to spend a little more, a midrange and possibly a fairway driver are a good addition.

Important: learn to master these four discs before you buy new ones. Most courses in NRW can be played very well with this modest set.

If you'd like to hold different models in your hand, that's often easier than just reading about them. Feel free to ask at [training](/en/training/) – many players are happy to let you try out a few discs.

## So where do I get discs?

There are various ways to buy discs, and it's worth comparing the options. Ask other players in your local disc golf community whether they have used discs for sale. Experienced players are often willing to sell or swap their old discs cheaply.

> Also take a look at [our price comparison](/en/preisvergleich/) to find the best deals on new discs and compare the different shops.

Online platforms such as eBay Kleinanzeigen, Facebook Marketplace and disc golf forums can also be good sources of used and new discs. Visit local disc golf tournaments, where vendors are often on site offering a wide range of discs for sale. This is a great opportunity to handle discs and see which ones best suit your playing style. By exploring these different options, you can find the best discs for your game and save money at the same time.

## Conclusion

The best disc golf discs for beginners are rarely the most spectacular ones. Slow, controllable models usually help you the most, because they let you learn the basics properly and avoid frustration.
