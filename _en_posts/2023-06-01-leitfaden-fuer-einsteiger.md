Anyone starting out in disc golf quickly ends up with the same questions: Which discs do I really need? Do I have to master different throwing styles straight away? And why does my disc never fly like everyone else's? The good news is: you don't need to master everything at once to get started. A few basics are enough to make your first rounds fun rather than frustrating.

## What is disc golf, anyway?

Disc golf works much like classic golf, except that it's played with flying discs instead of a ball and clubs. The aim is to get the disc into a basket in as few throws as possible. It's played on holes of different lengths, with obstacles and varying angles.

The appeal lies precisely in the fact that the sport is quick to pick up yet stays exciting for a long time. You can get started without much of a barrier to entry, but after just a few rounds you'll notice how much technique, feel and tactics go into what is actually a simple movement.

## What you really need to get started

Many beginners first think of a complete bag. In practice, you need much less. For your first rounds, you mainly need:

- a putter for short, controlled throws
- a midrange for most approaches and throws from the tee
- sturdy shoes that still grip on wet ground
- a towel if the course is damp or muddy

A fairway driver can come later, but isn't necessary at the start. Fast drivers in particular often lead you to think more about the disc than about your own throw. That's rarely helpful for beginners.

> For most beginners: two discs you can control are better than five you can't throw cleanly yet.

## Your first throws don't have to be spectacular

At first, disc golf often looks easier than it feels. Many people want to throw far straight away and underestimate how important a clean release is. Yet the first learning phase is actually quite rewarding: even small technical improvements make a noticeable difference.

For the first few weeks, three priorities help:

1. getting the disc out of your hand cleanly
2. throwing straight rather than far
3. developing a feel for speed and control

Many beginners throw too tensely. A relaxed throw then quickly turns into a hectic yank of the arm. It's better to have a calm stance, a clear target in mind and a throw where the disc leaves your hand cleanly. Distance will come later almost by itself.

## Which throws you should learn first

It's tempting to take on everything at once: backhand, forehand, run-up, flex lines, putting from every position. In reality, it makes more sense to build a small foundation first.

### 1. Standstill backhand

The classic backhand from a standing position is the best way in for many people. Without a run-up, you can focus better on timing, grip and release. If you can throw cleanly from a standstill, you'll usually have far fewer issues to fix once you add a run-up.

### 2. Putting from short range

Putting rarely decides how much fun your first round is, but developing a feel for short throws early on helps enormously. At the beginning, it's not about making everything from ten metres. It's more important to throw calmly and repeatably from short distances.

### 3. Controlled approaches

Many strokes aren't lost on the tee, but on throws between 20 and 60 metres. If you stay calm here and prefer to play safely in front of the basket, you'll quickly save a lot of throws.

## Mistakes beginners often make

A few typical beginner mistakes come up almost every time. If you know them, you'll spare yourself unnecessary frustration.

### Buying discs that are too fast

Fast drivers look exciting, but rarely help at the start. When your technique isn't there yet, slower discs usually fly with more control and often even further.

### Too much power instead of clean technique

More power doesn't automatically mean better throws. Especially in your first rounds, a calm, clean movement achieves much more.

### Wanting to learn everything at once

Backhand, forehand, putting, run-up, special throws: all of that comes later. A few basics are enough to get started.

### Only focusing on distance

Throwing the disc 20 metres further doesn't help much if it then lands 30 metres away from the target. Control is more important than distance at the beginning.

## Course etiquette is part of it from day one

Disc golf is straightforward, but not without rules. A pleasant start also depends on how you behave on the course. You should get used to a few things early on:

- be considerate of other players and walkers
- only throw when the area is clear
- play at a good pace without rushing
- leave the course clean

If you're unsure about rules or how things work at the club, you'll find the most important answers in our [FAQ](/en/faq/).

## How to make sensible progress as a beginner

The biggest mistake after your first motivated rounds is often wanting too much too quickly. New discs, new technique tips, new videos, new throws. That feels productive, but often slows you down.

This order usually works better:

1. play a few relaxed rounds
2. build repeatable throws with just a few discs
3. work specifically on short putts and straight throws
4. then get feedback from other players

That's exactly why it often makes sense not to play only on your own. ## Conclusion

Disc golf seems technical at first, but at its core it's a very accessible sport. You don't need lots of equipment, a complicated setup or perfect technique for your first round. What matters more is starting with a manageable amount of gear, focusing on control rather than power and giving yourself time for the basics.
